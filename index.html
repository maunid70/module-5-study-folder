          The Bohr model, live

          Click any orbit to move the electron there. Falling down emits a photon; jumping up absorbs one. Every equation on the right recalculates for the jump you just made, step by step.

          Try:

          1. 1 · Energy of each level

            The electron’s energy depends only on n (and Z). It is negative because a bound electron has less energy than a free one.

          2. 2 · Change in energy

            Final minus initial. The arrow on the ladder is ΔE.

          3. 3 · Energy of the photon

            The photon carries exactly the energy gap, never part of it. That is why only certain lines appear.

          4. 4 · Wavelength, λ

            Distance from one crest to the next. Bigger energy, shorter λ.

          5. 5 · Frequency, ν

            How many crests pass a point each second. All light travels at c, so shorter waves must pass more often.

          6. 6 · Per mole

            One photon’s energy × Avogadro’s number, for one mole of atoms making the same jump.

          The photon as a wave

          Every wave here moves at the same speed, c. Watch the line in the middle: the shorter the wavelength, the more crests cross it each second. Wavelengths are drawn on a stretched scale so ultraviolet and infrared both fit.

          The highest-yield facts

          1. Exceptions: Cr = [Ar]4s13d5 and Cu = [Ar]4s13d10.
          2. Cations lose the highest-n electrons first: Fe2+ = [Ar]3d6, because 4s leaves before 3d.
          3. Ionization energy dips: B < Be (the 2p electron is higher in energy) and O < N (a paired p electron repels). The same happens with Al < Mg and S < P.
          4. Successive ionization energies: the big jump means you have reached the core. The number of electrons removed before it gives the group.
          5. Electron affinity: Cl releases more energy than F, because F is small and its added electron is crowded.
          6. Isoelectronic species: more protons means smaller, so O2− > F− > Na+ > Mg2+.
          7. Photoelectric effect: brighter light ejects more electrons, not faster ones. Only the frequency changes the kinetic energy.
          8. Hydrogen series: ΔE = Efinal − Einitial, and negative means a photon is emitted. Lines ending at n = 1 are ultraviolet, at n = 2 visible, at n = 3 infrared.
          9. Units: convert nm to m (× 10−9) before using c = λν or E = hc/λ. J per photon × NA ÷ 1000 gives kJ/mol.
          10. Quantum numbers: l runs from 0 to n − 1 and ml from −l to +l. A subshell has 2l + 1 orbitals, and a shell holds 2n2 electrons.
          11. Hund’s rule and magnetism: fill orbitals singly before pairing. Any unpaired electron makes a substance paramagnetic: O2 is paramagnetic, N2 is diamagnetic.
          12. PES: peaks further left have higher binding energy and sit closer to the nucleus. Peak height is the number of electrons.
          13. Explaining trends: use Zeff, core electrons (shielding) and distance, which is Coulomb’s law. Never “it wants an octet.”
          14. Beer–Lambert: A is proportional to c, so read an unknown off the standards’ line and keep the same cuvette.

            Equation sheet

            Constants are given on quizzes and tests. Know what each variable means and which equation links the two quantities you have.

            c = λν
            Speed of light = wavelength × frequency. λ in meters, ν in s−1 (Hz).
            E = hν = hc/λ
            Energy of one photon, in joules.
            En = −2.178 × 10−18 J (Z2/n2)
            Bohr energy level. One-electron species only (H, He+, Li2+). Z = nuclear charge.
            ΔE = Efinal − Einitial
            Negative ΔE: photon emitted (electron falls). Positive: photon absorbed.
            1/λ = R(1/nlow2 − 1/nhigh2)
            Balmer–Rydberg equation for hydrogen. R = 1.097 × 107 m−1.
            KE = Ephoton − Φ
            Photoelectric effect. Φ is the work function (minimum energy to free an electron). KE = ½mv2.
            λ = h/mv
            de Broglie wavelength of a moving particle. m in kg, v in m/s.
            Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π
            Heisenberg uncertainty principle. p = mv.
            A = εbc
            Beer–Lambert law. ε in L/(mol·cm), b = path length in cm, c in mol/L.
            A = −log T
            Absorbance from transmittance (T as a fraction). The colorimeter in the penny lab reads A directly.
            M1V1 = M2V2
            Dilution: the concentration of each penny-lab standard.
            Zeff ≈ Z − S
            Effective nuclear charge. S = number of core (shielding) electrons.
            F ∝ q1q2/r2
            Coulomb’s law. Bigger charges or smaller distance mean a stronger attraction.
            c = 2.998 × 108 m/s
            Speed of light.
            h = 6.626 × 10−34 J·s
            Planck’s constant.
            NA = 6.022 × 1023 mol−1
            Per photon × NA = per mole. Divide by 1000 for kJ/mol.
            1 nm = 10−9 m
            Visible light is about 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red).

            Trends at a glance

            PropertyAcross a period (→)Down a group (↓)Why
            Atomic radiusDecreasesIncreasesAcross: more protons, same core shielding, so higher Zeff pulls the shell in. Down: a new shell farther out.
            First ionization energyIncreases (dips at B and O)DecreasesHigher Zeff and smaller r mean a stronger Coulomb attraction to the electron removed.
            Electron affinityGenerally more energy released (exceptions: Be, N, noble gases)No clean trendA higher Zeff attracts the added electron more strongly.
            ElectronegativityIncreasesDecreasesSame reasoning as ionization energy, for electrons shared in a bond. F is highest.
            Metallic characterDecreasesIncreasesLow ionization energy means electrons are given up or shared easily.
            Ion sizeCation smaller than its atom; anion larger. Isoelectronic ions: more protons, smaller ion.Fewer electrons per proton (or a lost shell) pulls the cloud in.
